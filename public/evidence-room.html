How to recognize grace

You do not need a famous example to spot it. In every case below, look for these five things.

  1. No audience. The act did not need to be seen to be worth doing. See Sir Nicholas Winton, Larry Stewart, the original Secret Santa, Chuck Feeney.
  2. Nothing coming back. The giver asked for no credit, no payment, no return. See Anonymous Kmart layaway donors, 2011, Tiger Mask backpacks, Ronald Read.
  3. A real cost. Time, money, safety or comfort was given up, not just spare change. See Irena Sendler, Zura Karuhimbi, Le Chambon-sur-Lignon.
  4. Strangers. The help went to people the giver would never meet again. See A stranger's kidney starts a chain of 60 operations, Anonymous marrow and stem cell donors, Las Patronas.
  5. It keeps going. It was not one gesture. It was years of it, often by whole communities. See Luis Soriano and the Biblioburro, The Golden Temple langar, Abdul Sattar Edhi.

The cases

Rescue Prague to London, Czech Republic

Sir Nicholas Winton

Organized the rescue of 669 children from Prague and told no one for about fifty years
In 1939 Winton, a London stockbroker, organized eight trains that carried 669 children from Nazi-threatened Czechoslovakia to Britain and found foster homes for them. He told almost no one about it for about fifty years. The story became public in 1988, when his wife found documents about the children in their attic.
Grace to look for: Do the whole job, then leave the credit on the table.

  • Nicholas Winton and the rescue of children from Czechoslovakia, 1938-1939 United States Holocaust Memorial Museum
  • Rotarian Sir Nicholas Winton dies peacefully aged 106 Rotary in Great Britain and Ireland
Where the sources differ: Sources differ on what his wife found: a scrapbook in most accounts, a briefcase of lists and letters in the Rotary account. He became very well known after 1988, so the unseen part describes the decades before.
Rescue Warsaw, Poland

Irena Sendler

Smuggled children out of the Warsaw Ghetto and buried their real names in jars
Sendler, a Polish social worker, helped get about 2,500 Jewish children out of the Warsaw Ghetto and placed them with families and institutions. She recorded their real names in code and buried the lists in jars under a neighbor's apple tree. The Gestapo arrested and tortured her in 1943, and she did not reveal the children's hiding places.
Grace to look for: Keep the record so the people are not lost, even when keeping it is dangerous.
2 independent sources Check it yourself
  • Irena Sendler Jewish Virtual Library
  • Irena Sendler Raoul Wallenberg Foundation
Where the sources differ: The figure of 2,500 is a commonly cited estimate. Her story reached a wide audience late, after Kansas students researched it for a 2000 play. She said she did not consider herself a hero.
Rescue Kaunas, Lithuania

Chiune Sugihara

Issued thousands of transit visas in Lithuania in 1940 and rarely spoke of it
In summer 1940 Sugihara, Japan's consul in Kaunas, Lithuania, issued transit visas to Jewish refugees after Tokyo refused exceptions. The visas let thousands travel across the Soviet Union to Japan. His son said he rarely talked about it, and his story became known after an Israeli diplomat found him in 1968.
Grace to look for: Use the one small power your job gives you, however small it looks.

Where the sources differ: Counts differ: 2,139 names on his list (Sugihara House), 2,178 (USHMM), at least 2,000 (JTA), and 6,000 or more people helped once families are counted. Sources also differ on whether he defied explicit orders.
Rescue Kaunas, Lithuania

Jan Zwartendijk

A Dutch businessman whose "Curacao visas" opened an escape route, remembered by many only as "Mr. Philips Radio"
In 1940 Zwartendijk, a Dutch Philips manager acting as honorary consul in Kaunas, issued papers saying no visa was needed to enter Curacao and other Dutch territories. These let Jewish refugees apply for Japanese transit visas. For years his name was unknown to many of the people he helped.
Grace to look for: Help that is only a piece of paper can still open a door.
2 independent sources Check it yourself
  • Jan Zwartendijk United States Holocaust Memorial Museum
  • Former Dutch honorary consul in Kaunas receives posthumous award for saving Jews LRT, Lithuanian public broadcaster
Where the sources differ: Counts differ: more than 2,000 visas (LRT) and at least 2,345 recovered inscriptions. He was recognized by Yad Vashem in 1997 and by the Dutch government in 2023, after his death in 1976.
Giving New York, United States

Chuck Feeney

Gave away about eight billion dollars in secret
Chuck Feeney, co-founder of Duty Free Shoppers, moved his stake in the business to a foundation that became The Atlantic Philanthropies. It gave to universities, hospitals and other causes without his name on the gifts, and many recipients did not know where the money came from. The foundation spent down its funds by 2016.
Grace to look for: Give where your name never appears, and ask for nothing back.

Giving Kalamazoo, Michigan, United States

The Kalamazoo Promise

Anonymous donors pay college tuition for a whole school district
In November 2005 the Kalamazoo Public Schools announced a scholarship program funded by anonymous donors. It pays tuition and fees at public Michigan colleges and universities for district graduates, from 65 percent to 100 percent depending on how long the student attended. The donors have stayed unidentified.
Grace to look for: Give to people you will never meet, without knowing who they will become.

Giving Kansas City area, United States

Larry Stewart, the original Secret Santa

Handed strangers $100 bills for about 26 years before revealing his name
Beginning in 1979, Larry Stewart of the Kansas City area gave cash to strangers each Christmas season, later in $100 bills, and kept his identity secret for about a quarter century. He gave away more than a million dollars. He revealed his name in late 2006 while being treated for cancer, to encourage others to be kind, and died in early 2007.
Grace to look for: The hand-off to a stranger, then the walk away.

Where the sources differ: Totals differ: more than $1 million (KCUR, NBC) and about $1.3 million (others). He became public by choice, so he was anonymous for most of the giving, not all of it.
Giving Indianapolis and elsewhere, United States

Anonymous Kmart layaway donors, 2011

Strangers quietly paid off other families' toy layaways
In December 2011, unnamed people at Kmart stores in Indianapolis, Omaha, Missoula and elsewhere paid off layaway balances for strangers, mostly toys and children's clothes. In Indianapolis a widow paid for dozens of people and asked them only to remember her husband, without giving her name.
Grace to look for: Pay a stranger's balance and ask only that they remember someone else.

Giving Brattleboro, Vermont, United States

Ronald Read

A gas station attendant and janitor quietly left six million dollars to a hospital and a library
Ronald Read worked at a gas station and later as a janitor in Brattleboro, Vermont, and was known as private and frugal. After he died in 2014, his estate gave $4.8 million to Brattleboro Memorial Hospital and $1.2 million to the Brooks Memorial Library. Neighbors did not know he had built a large fortune by picking stocks.
Grace to look for: Live plainly, give quietly, and let the gift speak after you.

Where the sources differ: This was a bequest revealed after his death, not giving he did while alive. His attorney put the estate at about $8 million.
Everyday strangers Eleven US states, United States

A stranger's kidney starts a chain of 60 operations

One donor with no one to give to set off 30 transplants across 17 hospitals
In 2011 Rick Ruzzamenti donated a kidney to a stranger, starting a chain run by the nonprofit National Kidney Registry. It grew to 30 donors and 30 recipients, 60 operations at 17 hospitals in 11 states over about four months. Many of the donors gave to a stranger because they could not give to a loved one, who then received a match through the exchange.
Grace to look for: Say yes first, with no one waiting for you at the other end.
2 independent sources Check it yourself

  • 60 lives, 30 kidneys, all linked (New York Times reporting) NBC News
Where the sources differ: Both sources describe the same chain, one from the organizer and one a news report. Most participants were not publicly named.
Everyday strangers Everywhere

Why people give a kidney to a stranger

Interview studies of people who donate a kidney to someone they will never know
A 2013 interview study of six prospective altruistic kidney donors found they described the urge to donate as compelling and mostly rejected personal gain as a motive. A 2020 abstract from UCLA interviewed 31 non-directed donors and grouped their reasons into inspiration from media, recognition of the broad need, and personal experience of loss.
Grace to look for: Ask what moves people when nothing comes back, then listen.
2 independent sources Check it yourself
  • Challenor and Watts (2013), Health, City University of London repository City, University of London
  • Why donate to a stranger? Motivations of 31 non-directed altruistic living kidney donors American Transplant Congress
Where the sources differ: Both are small studies using different methods. The 2020 source is a conference abstract, not a journal article.
Everyday strangers Registries worldwide

Anonymous marrow and stem cell donors

Registry donors give to patients they are not allowed to identify for at least a year
Marrow and stem cell donors through registries such as NMDP and DKMS give to a patient they do not know. In the United States the two must stay anonymous for at least a year, with any letters passing through the transplant center. After that they can meet only if both agree in writing.
Grace to look for: Give your body's gift to someone you may never be allowed to meet.

Everyday strangers 355 cities in 40 countries

Lost wallets in 40 countries

Strangers returned lost wallets more often when more money was inside
In a 2019 field experiment published in Science, researchers left more than 17,000 wallets with staff at banks, museums and other places in 355 cities across 40 countries. People were more likely to try to return a wallet when it held more money, and that held in nearly every country.
Grace to look for: Hand it back, even when no one is watching and it would be easy not to.
3 independent sources Check it yourself
  • Civic honesty around the globe (Science, 2019) Science
  • Study: people more likely to return lost wallet with more money Sci.News
  • Scientists dropped 17,000 wallets to understand how honest we really are ScienceAlert
Where the sources differ: The outcome was whether the recipient contacted the supposed owner, not a confirmed return. The $94 condition was run in only three countries. The study did not test refusing a reward.
Rescue Denmark to Sweden, Denmark

The Rescue of the Danish Jews

Ordinary Danes ferried about seven thousand people to Sweden in fishing boats in October 1943
In October 1943, when German authorities began hunting Denmark's Jews, ordinary Danes hid refugees in houses near the shore and led them to fishing boats that carried them across the water to neutral Sweden. About seven thousand people escaped over roughly a month.
Grace to look for: A whole country decided, without a meeting, to do the same right thing.
3 independent sources Check it yourself

Where the sources differ: Counts differ: about 7,200 lives saved (Nordic Museum) versus around 7,000 escaped with just under 500 caught and deported (Danish Jewish Museum).
Rescue Southern France, France

Le Chambon-sur-Lignon

A mountain village in France sheltered thousands of refugees and kept the secret for years
In and around Le Chambon-sur-Lignon in southern France, villagers, led by Pastor Andre Trocme and others, hid and protected refugees, many of them children, from the Vichy authorities and the Germans. Nobody asked who was Jewish and who was not.
Grace to look for: Open the door first and ask the questions never.
2 independent sources Check it yourself
  • Le Chambon-sur-Lignon United States Holocaust Memorial Museum
  • Le Chambon-sur-Lignon: a remarkable tale of wartime bravery France Today
Where the sources differ: The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives about 5,000 Jews for the whole effort and 3,000 to 3,500 among those fleeing the Vichy authorities and the Germans. France Today says only "thousands."
Rescue Albania, Albania

Albania and the besa

A small country that sheltered Jews under a code of honor and lost almost none of its own
In Albania, a mostly Muslim country, many families sheltered Jewish refugees and Albanian Jews under the besa, a code of honor that obliges a host to protect a guest. One scholarly source says almost all native Jews survived and thousands of refugees were protected. The story only began to come out after communism fell in the 1990s.
Grace to look for: A promise to a guest is stronger than a rule from a stranger.
3 independent sources Check it yourself

  • What made Muslim Albanians risk their lives to save Jews from the Holocaust? Jewish Telegraphic Agency
  • BESA: a code of honor Yad Vashem UK
Where the sources differ: Counts differ: the Yad Vashem exhibition notes estimates of 600 to 1,800 Jewish refugees entering the country, while the EHRI article speaks of thousands protected overall. No accurate figures exist.
Rescue Bulgaria, Bulgaria

Bulgaria's citizens, 1943

Church leaders, a deputy speaker and ordinary people stopped a deportation order
In March 1943 Bulgaria, allied with Germany, had ordered the deportation of its Jews. Church leaders, a deputy speaker of parliament named Dimitar Peshev, and many ordinary citizens protested, and the government rescinded the order. The roughly 48,000 Jews of Bulgaria proper were not deported.
Grace to look for: Many voices at once can say no to an order that one voice cannot.
3 independent sources Check it yourself
  • Bulgaria United States Holocaust Memorial Museum
  • Remembering the rescue of Bulgaria's Jews Anti-Defamation League
  • How the Jewish population of Bulgaria was saved from the Nazis The Algemeiner
Where the sources differ: Bulgarian authorities did deport about 4,000 Jews from Greek Thrace and others from territories they occupied (USHMM), so this rescue covers Jews inside Bulgaria's core provinces only. Sources credit different groups, from church leaders to parliamentarians to citizens.
Giving Gunma Prefecture, Japan

Tiger Mask backpacks

Someone left school backpacks at a children's home signed with a cartoon hero's name, and hundreds followed
On Christmas Day 2010 someone left ten leather school backpacks at a child guidance center in Gunma Prefecture, Japan, with a note asking that they be used for the children, signed with the name of a manga hero, Naoto Date (Tiger Mask). It inspired hundreds of similar anonymous gifts around Japan, and in 2020 an Okayama donor who signed as "Okayama's Tiger Mask" made a final delivery after five years.
Grace to look for: Take a borrowed name and let the gift stay anonymous.

Giving Karachi, Pakistan

Abdul Sattar Edhi

Built a welfare network that served people free of cost and lived in two rooms beside it
Edhi, from Karachi, set up a welfare foundation almost six decades before his death, overseeing it with his wife Bilquis. It provided help to the poor, free of cost and without discrimination. He lived with his family in a two-room apartment next to its headquarters and was given Pakistan's first state funeral in 30 years.
Grace to look for: Serve everyone who comes, and keep your own life small.

Giving Amritsar, India

The Golden Temple langar

A kitchen that feeds up to a hundred thousand people a day, free, to anyone who comes
At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, India, a free community kitchen called a langar serves meals to as many as about 100,000 people a day, regardless of faith, background or gender. Hundreds of volunteers cook and serve, about 500 a day by one count.
Grace to look for: A meal where no one asks who you are, and everyone sits at the same level.

Giving Taitung, Taiwan

Chen Shu-chu

A vegetable seller in Taiwan gave away nearly ten million Taiwan dollars of her savings
Chen Shu-chu has sold vegetables in a stall in Taitung's central market since 1963. Out of her modest living she donated nearly NT$10 million (about $320,000), including money for a children's fund, a library at a school she attended, and a local orphanage. A teacher takes books to remote villages on two donkeys

Rescue Mexico City, Mexico

Los Topos of Mexico City

Volunteers who dig by hand for strangers after earthquakes, since 1985
Los Topos, "the Moles," are a volunteer search and rescue group formed after Mexico's 1985 earthquake killed more than 5,000 people. They have joined about 70 rescue missions at home and abroad. After the 2017 earthquake, ordinary people formed lines to carry buckets of debris away from damaged sites.
Grace to look for: Show up with your hands before anyone asks.

Everyday strangers La Patrona, Veracruz, Mexico

Las Patronas

Two sisters threw food to hungry strangers on a passing train and kept doing it for decades
In February 1995 two sisters in La Patrona, Veracruz, Mexico, waiting for a train to pass, tossed the food they had just bought to migrants riding on top of the cars who called out that they were hungry. That became Las Patronas, a volunteer-run group that provides food, supplies and shelter to migrants traveling north.
Grace to look for: Feed the person in front of you, today, with what you have in your hands.

Rescue Ruhango district, Rwanda

Zura Karuhimbi

An old healer in Rwanda hid about a hundred people from the killers in 1994
During the 1994 genocide in Rwanda, Zura Karuhimbi, an older woman known as a traditional healer, sheltered about a hundred people in her home. By several accounts she used her reputation as a witchdoctor, acting as if possessed, to frighten off the militias. She died in 2018 at the age of 109.

Everyday strangers Gander, Newfoundland, Canada

Gander and the stranded passengers of 9/11

A town of about 10,000 took in thousands of strangers when U.S. airspace closed
When U.S. airspace closed on September 11, 2001, 38 airliners landed at the small airport in Gander, Newfoundland. Roughly 6,600 to 7,000 passengers and crew were stuck for about three days in a town of around 10,000. Residents and nearby communities turned schools, churches and halls into shelters and opened their homes to the visitors.
Grace to look for: Open the door for people you will never see again.

Everyday strangers Western Front, Flanders, Belgium

The Christmas Truce of 1914

Soldiers on opposite sides paused the war on their own, with no order to do so
On Christmas Eve 1914 soldiers in the trenches began informal ceasefires, starting with carols sung and shouted across the lines. In places, men then met in no man's land, swapped small gifts and buried their dead together. The truces were local and unofficial, mostly between British and German troops. The war went on for almost four more years.
Grace to look for: Put the weapon down first and see who meets you halfway.

  • The Christmas Truce: when the fighting paused in World War I World History Encyclopedia

The truces were scattered, not front-wide, and no reliable total of participants exists. The location shown is the approximate center of the Ypres Salient area, not one spot.
Everyday strangers Harlem, New York, United States

Wesley Autrey

A construction worker jumped onto subway tracks and covered a stranger as a train passed
On January 2, 2007, Cameron Hollopeter, a film student, had a seizure on a Manhattan subway platform and fell onto the tracks. Wesley Autrey, a construction worker, jumped down and, unable to lift him out, pushed him into the drainage trough between the rails and lay on top of him. The train passed over them and both men survived; Hollopeter was bruised and hospitalized.
Grace to look for: Act in the one second you are given, before you can talk yourself out of it.

Rescue Amsterdam, Netherlands

Miep Gies

An Amsterdam office worker helped hide eight people and kept Anne Frank's diary safe
Miep Gies worked for Otto Frank and was one of the helpers who supplied the people hiding in the Secret Annex in Amsterdam. After the arrest on 4 August 1944 she and a colleague gathered Anne's notebooks and papers and kept them, unread, in a drawer. She gave them to Otto Frank, the only survivor of the eight, and the diary was published in 1947. Gies died in 2010 at the age of 100.
Grace to look for: Keep the thing that matters safe, and ask for no credit.

Rescue Florence, Italy

Gino Bartali

A Tour de France champion hid forged papers in his bicycle to save Jews, and rarely spoke of it
Bartali won the Tour de France in 1938 and 1948. After Germany occupied Italy in 1943 he joined a rescue network and carried forged identity documents between cities, hidden in his bicycle under cover of training rides. He also hid a Jewish family in his cellar. He almost never spoke of it, and Yad Vashem honored him as Righteous Among the Nations after his death in 2000.
Grace to look for: Do the dangerous errand under the cover of an ordinary one.

Rescue Berlin, Germany

Mohamed Helmy

An Egyptian doctor in Berlin hid a Jewish family and kept all four alive
Helmy, an Egyptian physician born in Khartoum, lived in Berlin, where the Nazis barred him from public-health work as a non-Aryan. When Berlin's Jews were being deported, he hid a young woman, Anna Boros, in his cabin and arranged hiding places for her mother, stepfather and grandmother. With a German friend he kept all four alive. Yad Vashem named him the first Arab or Egyptian rescuer honored as Righteous Among the Nations.
Grace to look for: Shelter the person the law has turned against.

USHMM dates the honor to March 2013 and Yad Vashem's press release came in September 2013. The record rests largely on letters from the family found in archives later.
Rescue Bordeaux, France

Aristides de Sousa Mendes

A Portuguese consul disobeyed his government and issued visas to refugees, and was punished for it
In June 1940 Portugal's government had forbidden visas for refugees, but its consul general in Bordeaux, Aristides de Sousa Mendes, issued them anyway, working for days with two of his sons. Lisbon recalled him, a disciplinary panel dismissed him, and he died poor in 1954. Yad Vashem named him Righteous Among the Nations in 1966, and Portugal rehabilitated him in 1988.
Grace to look for: Break the rule that says to turn people away, and accept the cost.

Rescue Budapest, Hungary

Giorgio Perlasca

An Italian businessman posed as a Spanish diplomat and protected thousands in Budapest
Perlasca, an Italian trader trapped in Budapest in 1944, stepped into the role of Spanish chief of the legation when the ambassador left in late November 1944, though he had no official authority. From December 1, 1944 to mid-January 1945 he ran the legation and kept issuing protective papers and housing to Jews. He told almost no one, even his family, until Hungarian survivors traced him to Padua in the 1980s. He died in 1992.
Grace to look for: Step into the empty role that someone has to fill.

Rescue Lagos, Nigeria

Dr. Ameyo Adadevoh

A Lagos doctor refused to release Nigeria's first Ebola patient, and died of it
In July 2014 Dr. Ameyo Stella Adadevoh, a senior physician at First Consultant Hospital in Lagos, suspected Ebola in a collapsed traveler from Liberia, ordered a test, and kept him isolated even though he demanded to leave and Liberian officials pressed for his release. Nigerian health officials then traced hundreds of contacts, and the WHO declared Nigeria free of Ebola on October 20, 2014. Adadevoh caught the disease and died in August 2014.

Louisiana boat owners rescue flood victims in Katrina and Harvey

Rescue Brussels, Belgium

Andrée Geulen

A Brussels teacher led hundreds of Jewish children to hiding places
Andrée Geulen was a young schoolteacher in Brussels when Jewish pupils began to be marked with yellow stars. She joined the Committee for the Defense of Jews and, between spring 1943 and September 1944, escorted more than 300 Jewish children to hiding places in Christian homes and monasteries, under false names. Yad Vashem named her Righteous Among the Nations in 1989, and she received honorary Israeli citizenship in 2007. She died in 2022 at age 100.
Grace to look for: Look for the ordinary job turned into a way to hide children.

Rescue St. Gallen, Switzerland

Paul Grüninger

A Swiss police captain let refugees in, was fired and convicted, then cleared
Paul Grüninger commanded the St. Gallen cantonal police. In 1938 and 1939, after the Swiss government closed the border to Jewish refugees, he let hundreds of them in, sometimes by backdating their entry dates. He was suspended in 1939, dismissed, and convicted in 1940 of breach of duty and document falsification, and he lived in poverty until his death in 1972. Yad Vashem honored him in 1971. In 1995 the St. Gallen district court reopened the case and acquitted him after his death.
Grace to look for: Look for the one who broke a rule to keep a person safe.

Rescue Kamaishi, Iwate Prefecture, Japan

The Miracle of Kamaishi

Teenagers ran first in the 2011 tsunami, and the little ones followed them to safety
When the March 11, 2011 earthquake struck, students of Kamaishi East Junior High School ran to higher ground without waiting for orders. Children and teachers of the neighboring Unosumai Elementary School followed, older students supported the younger ones, and all reached safety before the tsunami destroyed both schools and much of the town. About 99.8 percent of the roughly 3,000 school children in the city survived, after years of drills taught by Professor Toshitaka Katada.
Grace to look for: Look for the older kids who moved first and took the small hands.

Rescue Sarajevo, Bosnia and Herzegovina

Dervis Korkut

A museum librarian who hid the Sarajevo Haggadah from the Nazis
In 1942, when German officials came to the National Museum in Sarajevo for the Sarajevo Haggadah, a medieval Jewish manuscript, Dervis Korkut, a Muslim scholar who worked there as librarian and curator, took it out of the museum and kept it from them. It was hidden in a mosque outside the city and returned to the museum after the war. Korkut and his wife Servet also sheltered a young Jewish woman, Mira Papo, in their home, and Yad Vashem later recognized them as Righteous Among the Nations.
Grace to look for: Look for people who protect what belongs to others.

Thousands of strangers worked to free 13 trapped in a Thai cave, 2018
In June 2018 twelve boys and their soccer coach were trapped by flooding in the Tham Luang cave in northern Thailand. A consul on Rhodes who talked the Germans out of deporting Jews, 1944
Selahattin Ulkumen was the Turkish consul-general on Rhodes when German forces began deporting the island's Jews in July 1944. Rescue Budapest, Hungary

Raoul Wallenberg

A Swedish diplomat shielded Jews in Budapest with protective papers
Raoul Wallenberg reached Budapest on July 9, 1944, sent by Sweden at the request of the U.S. War Refugee Board. After learning about protective passes from Carl Lutz and others, he won permission for 1,500 and, by bribing officials, raised the figure to 15,000. After the Arrow Cross seized power in October 1944 he issued papers without distinction, had 32 buildings placed under Swedish protection, and pulled people out of death marches. Soviet soldiers took him away on January 17, 1945, and he was never seen again.
Grace to look for: Look for a stranger who spent his own money and nerve on strangers.

Rescue Warsaw, Poland

Żegota, the Council to Aid Jews

Poles and Jews ran a secret rescue group under Nazi occupation
Żegota was an underground rescue organization of Poles and Jews that operated in German-occupied Poland from December 4, 1942, to January 1945, with support from the Polish government-in-exile. Its members supplied false identity papers, found hiding places, and delivered money, food and medical help. Its children's section was led by Irena Sendler. Since autumn 1941, helping Jews in occupied Poland had carried the death penalty.
Grace to look for: Look for people who worked together across deep divides to save strangers.

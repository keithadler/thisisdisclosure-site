Country guide

Australia.

Australian law is made mostly by states and territories. The Northern Territory Criminal Code makes it an offence to callously fail to rescue a person urgently in need, and civil liability laws in several jurisdictions protect people who help in emergencies. The federal tax law allows deductions for gifts to listed recipients, and the federal government funded a National Strategy for Volunteering.

Is there a legal duty to help a stranger?

Varies by region

Section 155 of the Northern Territory Criminal Code Act 1983 makes it an offence for a person able to provide rescue, resuscitation, medical treatment, first aid or succour to a person urgently in need, whose life may be endangered if it is not provided, to callously fail to do so, with a maximum of 7 years imprisonment. The sources checked did not verify a comparable offence in other jurisdictions.

Criminal Code Act 1983 (NT), as in force at 12 October 2026 (Northern Territory Legislation)

Are people who help protected?

Varies by region

A 2007 Queensland Parliamentary Library brief describes Good Samaritan civil liability laws in all jurisdictions except Tasmania, including Part 8 of the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW), section 31B of the Wrongs Act 1958 (Vic) and section 8 of the Personal Injuries (Liabilities and Damages) Act (NT); current texts should be checked. In Queensland, section 16 of the Law Reform Act 1995 protects medical practitioners, nurses and prescribed persons giving emergency care in good faith without gross negligence and without fee, and the Civil Liability Act 2003 protects prescribed public safety entities and their workers under ss. 26 and 27.

One thing worth knowing

The Northern Territory is the one jurisdiction found here with a criminal offence for callously failing to rescue, paired with a separate offence for obstructing those who give aid.

What this does not mean

Rules differ by state and territory, and the Good Samaritan summary relies partly on a 2007 parliamentary brief and a Queensland statute version from 2017, so current law may differ.

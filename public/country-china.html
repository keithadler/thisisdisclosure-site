Country guide

China.

The Civil Code of the People's Republic of China (adopted 2020, in force January 1, 2021) includes a provision on emergency aid under which an aider who causes harm while voluntarily giving aid does not bear civil liability. The Charity Law (2016, amended 2023) and the State Council's Regulation of Volunteer Service (2017) set rules for and encourage charitable and volunteer activity, and national campaigns such as learning from Lei Feng are policy rather than law.

Is there a legal duty to help a stranger?

No general duty found

No general legal duty to help a stranger in danger was found in the Civil Code text reviewed. Article 184 addresses liability of those who do help, and Article 121 gives a person who voluntarily manages another's affairs to avoid harm the right to request repayment of necessary expenses. This guide did not review criminal or administrative law, so it does not claim no duty exists anywhere.

Are people who help protected?

Yes

Civil Code Article 184 provides that where voluntarily carrying out emergency assistance causes harm to the person receiving assistance, the aider does not bear civil liability. By its words it addresses civil liability only, and it is silent on criminal liability. Article 183 separately addresses compensation when someone is harmed while protecting another's civil rights.

One thing worth knowing

China's Civil Code puts an emergency-aid liability exemption in the national civil code itself (Article 184), next to a rule letting voluntary helpers recover necessary expenses (Article 121).

What this does not mean

The English texts cited are private translations, not official English versions; the Chinese text controls. The Charity Law translation shown is the 2016 version and the law was amended in 2023. Policy campaigns and official media statements are not law, and nothing here speaks to how the provisions are applied in practice.

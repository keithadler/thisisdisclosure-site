Cheat sheet for everyday life.

Most of the test is small, public and over in five seconds.

  1. Move in the first five seconds.
    When you see someone struggling (bags, a stroller, lost, crying), you have about five seconds before the moment passes. Go.
    Say"Can I help with that?"
  2. Pay for a stranger and leave.
    Coffee, a meal, a bus fare. Be gone before they can thank you. Nothing comes back. That is the point.
  3. Be the paramedic.
    If someone is hurt in public, go toward them, call for help and stay until help arrives. Don't ask about the crime, the person or your training.
  4. Return what isn't yours.
    The wallet, the phone, the extra change. Leave a note: "Found by someone who hoped you would get this back."
  5. Let the other person finish.
    With someone you disagree with, repeat back what they said before you answer.
    Say"So what you're saying is..."
  6. Treat the one who can do nothing for you like the one who can.
    The waiter, the janitor, the intern, the stranger. Same voice, same eye contact, same patience.
  7. Do one kindness a day that nobody sees.
    Then notice what it does to your heart. That lift is the sign it was never for nothing.

The evidence behind this sheet

Experiments find that small acts of giving and kindness raise the giver's mood a little, and field data show that most bystanders do step in when someone is hurt. The effects are modest and vary by method, so treat these as low-cost habits, not cures.

Where the evidence is thin. Most kindness studies are short, use self-reports, and show small effects that shrink in some replications; the bystander footage covers street conflicts in three countries, not every setting. These sheets do not claim that any single act changes your life, and nothing here is health advice.
If you only do one thing this week
One kindness a day that nobody sees.

Cheat sheet for elders.

You have time and stories, the two things everyone else is short of.

  1. Be the one who notices.
    Learn the name of the cashier, the bus driver, the neighbor's kid. Use it. and wait for the whole answer.
    Say"How is your day going, really?"
  2. Make the call nobody makes.
    Every week, call one person who is alone. Have no agenda.
    Say"I was thinking about you."
  3. Give the lesson away.
    Offer a skill (cooking, fixing, saving money, getting through a bad year) to someone younger, free.
    Say"Want me to show you?"
  4. Tell the real story.
    Share one time you were badly wrong and what it cost. Honest beats impressive.
    Say"Here is what I would do differently."
  5. Give it now, not later.
    Hand over the thing you are saving (a tool, a book, money) to someone who needs it today. Don't attach a story to it.
  6. Stay with the grieving.
    Don't fix it. Sit.
    Say"I'm here. I'm not going anywhere."
  7. Close one old account.
    Forgive or apologize for one old thing. Say it, or if saying it isn't safe, write it down and let the page take it.

The evidence behind this sheet

Large reviews link strong social ties and volunteering in later life with longer survival and better mood, and one randomized program shows gains in older adults' sense of purpose. Most of that evidence is observational, so it shows association more than proof.

Where the evidence is thin. Most volunteering and social-tie findings are observational, so healthier people may simply volunteer more, and intergenerational program studies are small and uneven. The sheet's calls, apologies, storytelling and sitting with grief have little direct trial evidence, and nothing here is medical advice.
If you only do one thing this week
One call, one gift, one apology.

This is practical, not religious. I think what you show with your choices is what counts. Share it freely.
